Rules of Chess

Each piece moves in its own way. The red marks on the diagrams show where a piece can move or capture from the position shown.

Moving the pieces Special moves End of the game

Moving the pieces

Pawn

A pawn moves one square forward. On its first move, it may move two squares if both squares are empty. It captures one square diagonally forward and cannot move backward or jump over a piece.

Promotion and en passant are explained below.

Knight

A knight moves in an L shape: two squares in one direction and one to the side. It is the only piece that can jump over other pieces.

From the centre, it can reach up to eight squares.

Bishop

A bishop moves diagonally as far as it likes, provided nothing blocks its path. It always stays on squares of the same colour.

Rook

A rook moves along a rank or file: horizontally or vertically. It may travel any number of squares, but cannot jump over another piece.

Queen

A queen combines the moves of the rook and bishop. It moves along a rank, file, or diagonal for any number of squares, as long as its path is clear.

King

A king moves one square in any direction. It cannot move onto a square attacked by an opponent's piece. Keeping the king safe is the main aim of the game.

Castling is its special move.

Special moves

Castling

Castling moves the king two squares toward a rook, then places that rook on the square the king crossed. You can castle only if neither piece has moved, the squares between them are empty, the king is not in check, and it does not cross or land on an attacked square.

Pawn promotion

When a pawn reaches the farthest rank, it must become a queen, rook, bishop, or knight of the same colour. The new piece replaces the pawn as part of that move. A queen is the usual choice, but another piece can sometimes be more useful.

En passant

If an opposing pawn moves two squares from its starting rank and lands beside your pawn, you may capture it as though it had moved only one square. You must do this immediately on your next move, or the chance is lost.

End of the game

A player wins by checkmate: the opponent's king is under attack and has no legal way to escape. A game can also end in a draw. Common examples are:

  • Stalemate: the player to move has no legal move, but their king is not in check.
  • Threefold repetition: the same position occurs three times with the same player to move.
  • Fifty-move rule: each player has made 50 moves without a pawn move or a capture.
  • Insufficient material: neither player has enough pieces to force checkmate.
  • Agreement: both players agree to a draw.
